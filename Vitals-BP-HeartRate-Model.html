Measuring blood pressure & heart rate from a face video

MediIQ can estimate a patient's heart rate and blood pressure using only a laptop or phone camera — no cuff, no smartwatch, no finger clip. The patient looks at the camera for 45 seconds and the system reads the tiny colour changes in their skin caused by their heartbeat. This document explains every single step, in very simple words.

📹 45-second face scan 🧠 3-model AI ensemble 🫀 1D-ResNet trained on MIMIC-III 📊 XGBoost fallback system 🔒 Video never leaves the device ±5–8 bpm accuracy

If you read only one thing: the system captures 45 seconds of face video in the browser, sends only tiny 72×72 face thumbnails to a Python server, runs three different rPPG neural networks on them, blends their answers, and feeds the resulting pulse wave into a ResNet trained on hospital ICU data to predict blood pressure.

Basics

1 · What we built and why

The problem

In a normal doctor visit, a nurse measures blood pressure with an inflatable cuff and heart rate with a finger clip. But MediIQ is an online consultation platform — the patient is at home. Most people do not own a BP machine.

Without vitals, the doctor is working half-blind. Blood pressure changes the prescription: for example, some medicines are unsafe if the patient has high blood pressure.

The solution

Use the camera that everyone already has. When your heart beats, it pushes blood into your face. That extra blood changes your skin colour by a tiny amount — far too small for the human eye, but a camera sensor can measure it. Read that colour change over time and you get the patient's pulse.

The whole idea in one sentenceYour face is blushing and un-blushing with every heartbeat, thousands of times smaller than you can see. The camera measures that flicker, and AI turns it into heart rate and blood pressure.

What the patient experiences

2 · What is rPPG? (explained simply)

First: what is PPG?

PPG stands for photoplethysmography. Long word, simple meaning: measuring blood volume using light.

You have seen this. The little clip a nurse puts on your finger, or the green light on the back of a smartwatch — both shine light into your skin and measure how much bounces back. When your heart beats and blood fills the tissue, less light comes back. Plot that over time and you get a wavy line: the pulse waveform.

Now: what is rPPG?

rPPG = remote photoplethysmography. Same idea, but with no contact. Instead of a sensor touching the skin, we use an ordinary camera from a distance. The camera's normal light is enough.

Plain-word definitions to memorise PPG signal / BVP = the wavy line showing your pulse over time (BVP = Blood Volume Pulse — same thing).
rPPG = getting that wavy line from a camera instead of a contact sensor.
SNR / SQI = "signal quality" — how clean the wave is versus noise. Higher is better.

Why is it hard?

The pulse signal is extremely weak — roughly 1% of the skin's colour. Meanwhile, all of these are much stronger and ruin it:

  • Head movement — even small shifts change how light hits the face.
  • Lighting changes — a flickering bulb or a cloud passing a window.
  • Camera auto-adjustments — webcams constantly change brightness automatically.
  • Compression — video compression throws away subtle colour detail.

So the entire engineering challenge is: pull a 1% signal out of a very noisy recording. That is exactly what the algorithms and neural networks in this document do.

3 · Two systems: the old one and the new one

This is the most important section to understand, because MediIQ contains two complete vitals systems. The project started with the first one and later upgraded to the second.

LEGACY · fallback

System A — POS + XGBoost

How it works: a hand-written maths algorithm (POS) turns skin colour into a pulse wave, 15 numbers ("features") are measured from that wave, and 6 XGBoost models predict BP.

Endpoint: /api/v1/vitals/predict-rgb
Browser sends: average colour numbers only
Trained on: PPG-BP dataset (219 subjects)

CURRENT · production

System B — 3 Neural Nets + ResNet

How it works: three deep-learning rPPG models each extract a pulse wave from face images, their outputs are blended by quality, and a 1D-ResNet predicts BP from the blended wave.

Endpoint: /api/v1/lab/predict-faces
Browser sends: 72×72 face thumbnails
Trained on: RLAP + MIMIC-III (huge datasets)

Which one runs today?System B (the neural network one) is what the patient actually uses. You can prove it from the code: the patient's scan window imports predictVitalsDL, which posts to /api/v1/lab/predict-faces. System A is kept in the codebase as a documented fallback and is still fully working.

Why upgrade from A to B?

Problem with System AHow System B fixes it
POS is a fixed formula — it cannot learn. It treats every face and lighting the same.Neural networks learned from thousands of real videos, so they handle skin tones, lighting and motion far better.
Only 3 small skin patches were used, so most of the face was thrown away.The whole face image goes into the network, which decides for itself which pixels matter.
XGBoost was trained on only 219 people's finger recordings.ResNet was pretrained on MIMIC-III, a massive hospital ICU waveform database.
One algorithm — if it failed, everything failed.Three models run together; if one fails or gives a poor signal, the others carry the result.

5 · Step 1 — Capturing the video in the browser

Everything here happens on the patient's own device, in JavaScript. File: components/vitals/useVitalsCapture.ts.

The settings, and why each was chosen

SettingValueWhy this number
Target frame rate30 fpsStandard webcam speed. To measure a heartbeat up to ~180 bpm (3 beats/sec) you need well above 6 fps — 30 gives plenty of headroom.
Scan duration45 secondsIncreased from 30s. More frames = cleaner signal. 45s gives ~1,350 frames, enough for many stable heartbeats and good HRV estimates.
Face crop size72 × 72 pxIncreased from 36×36 — 4× more detail. Big enough for the networks to see skin texture, small enough to keep the upload tiny.
Steady-before-start3 secondsThe face must be well positioned continuously for 3s before recording begins — prevents starting mid-fidget.
Face-loss pause2 secondsIf the face disappears (patient looks away), frames stop being collected rather than recording garbage.
Minimum frames300= 30 fps × 10 s. Below this the scan is rejected with a helpful message instead of a bad reading.

How a single frame is processed

1
Draw the video frame to a hidden canvas

A <canvas> element is an off-screen drawing surface. We paint the current camera frame onto it so we can read its pixels.

2
Find the face with MediaPipe

MediaPipe FaceLandmarker is Google's face-tracking model, running inside the browser. It returns 468 landmark points — precise dots on the eyes, nose, jawline, forehead, cheeks, etc.

3
Assess quality

Checks the face is present, large enough, centred and not too tilted. Result is good, poor, or none — shown live to the patient so they can correct themselves.

4
Compute the face bounding box

Loop through all 468 points to find min/max X and Y — that rectangle is the face. Then add 25% padding on every side, because useful blood-flow signal lives at the edges (forehead, jaw, neck).

5
Resize to 72×72 and store raw RGB

The cropped face is drawn into a 72×72 canvas. We then read the pixels and keep only R, G, B (dropping the alpha/transparency channel), giving 72 × 72 × 3 = 15,552 bytes per frame.

The crop logic (simplified from useVitalsCapture.ts)
// 1. Find the face rectangle from all 468 landmarks
for (const lm of landmarks) {
  xMin = Math.min(xMin, lm.x * W);   yMin = Math.min(yMin, lm.y * H);
  xMax = Math.max(xMax, lm.x * W);   yMax = Math.max(yMax, lm.y * H);
}

// 2. Add 25% padding around it
const pad = 0.25;
const sx = xMin - faceW * pad;      const sy = yMin - faceH * pad;
const sw = faceW * (1 + 2 * pad);   const sh = faceH * (1 + 2 * pad);

// 3. Squash that region into a 72x72 canvas
octx.drawImage(canvas, sx, sy, sw, sh, 0, 0, 72, 72);

// 4. Keep only R,G,B (skip the alpha byte)
rgb[i*3] = imgData.data[i*4];      // R
rgb[i*3+1] = imgData.data[i*4+1];  // G
rgb[i*3+2] = imgData.data[i*4+2];  // B

Why crop the face instead of sending the whole video?

  • Privacy — the background, room, and other people are never captured.
  • Size — a 45-second raw video is tens of megabytes; the crops are a few MB and compress to far less.
  • Accuracy — the networks were trained on cropped, aligned faces, so we must feed them the same format.
  • Stability — cropping to the tracked face means small head movements do not shift the image content.

6 · Step 2 — Sending the frames to the server

After 45 seconds we have roughly 1,350 frames × 15,552 bytes ≈ 21 MB of raw pixel data. Sending that as-is would be slow, so two things happen:

1
Gzip compression

Uses the browser's built-in CompressionStream("gzip"). Face crops compress extremely well because neighbouring frames look nearly identical. This typically cuts the size by a large factor.

2
Base64 encoding

Converts the compressed binary into plain text so it can travel inside normal JSON. (Base64 adds about 33% size, but makes the request simple and firewall-friendly.)

The request body (lib/vitalsApi.ts → predictVitalsDL)
POST /api/v1/lab/predict-faces
Content-Type: application/json

{
  "face_frames_b64": "H4sIAAAAAAAA/…",   // gzipped + base64 face stack
  "n_frames":        1350,
  "crop_size":       72,
  "fps":             29.7,                // the REAL measured fps, not assumed
  "demographics": { "age": 45, "sex": "M", "height": 175, "weight": 78 }
}
Small but important detailThe browser sends the actual measured frame rate (frames captured ÷ seconds elapsed), not a hard-coded 30. Real webcams drift — a laptop under load might deliver 26.4 fps. Since heart rate is calculated from timing, using the wrong fps would give a wrong heart rate. This one line protects the accuracy of the whole result.

7 · Step 3 — Server decodes and cleans the frames

File: ml/api/lab_router.py. The FastAPI server reverses the packing:

Decoding back into an image stack
raw_bytes    = base64.b64decode(req.face_frames_b64)
decompressed = gzip.decompress(raw_bytes)
faces        = np.frombuffer(decompressed, dtype=np.uint8) \
                 .reshape(req.n_frames, 72, 72, 3)

# Result: a 4-D array (frames, height, width, colour)

Temporal frame averaging — a clever noise trick

Before the AI sees anything, consecutive frames are averaged in pairs: frame 1 and 2 become one frame, 3 and 4 become one, and so on.

Pair averaging
n_pairs  = faces.shape[0] // 2
averaged = (faces[0::2][:n_pairs] + faces[1::2][:n_pairs]) / 2.0
effective_fps = req.fps / 2.0        # 30 fps → 15 fps

# 1350 frames @ 30fps  →  675 frames @ 15fps

Why does this help?

Camera sensors add random noise to every frame. Random noise partly cancels itself when you average, but the real pulse signal is not random — it is nearly the same in both frames, so it survives. The result is a cleaner signal.

The trade-off (good interview answer)Halving the frame rate to 15 fps means the fastest heartbeat we can still measure correctly drops. By the sampling rule (you need at least 2 samples per cycle), 15 fps can still capture up to 7.5 beats per second = 450 bpm — far above any human heart rate. So we lose nothing real and gain a cleaner signal. Note that effective_fps is then used everywhere downstream, so all timing stays correct.

8 · Step 4 — The three rPPG neural networks ⭐

This is the heart of the new system. Instead of one algorithm, MediIQ runs three different deep-learning models on the exact same face frames, through the open-rppg library.

The ensemble definition (lab_router.py)
_ENSEMBLE_NAMES = ["FacePhys.rlap", "PhysNet.rlap", "EfficientPhys.rlap"]

# Loaded once, then cached in memory for every later request
for name in _ENSEMBLE_NAMES:
    _rppg_models[name] = rppg.Model(name)

What each model is

1 PhysNet ›

Type: a 3D convolutional neural network (3D-CNN).

Simple explanation: a normal CNN looks at one image at a time. A 3D CNN looks at a stack of frames together, so it can see how pixels change over time — which is exactly what a pulse is. It slides a 3D filter through (height × width × time) and learns to output the pulse waveform directly.

Strength: excellent at picking up subtle, smooth colour rhythms. It is one of the classic, well-proven rPPG architectures.

2 EfficientPhys ›

Type: an efficient CNN built for speed.

Simple explanation: designed to give good rPPG results using far less computing power. It works largely on the difference between consecutive frames rather than the raw frames, which naturally removes anything static (the background, skin tone, moles) and keeps only what is changing — the blood flow.

Strength: fast, light, and robust to lighting differences. Good on CPU-only servers.

3 FacePhys ›

Type: a face-specialised rPPG network.

Simple explanation: tuned specifically for facial physiological measurement, learning which regions of a face carry the strongest blood-flow signal (forehead and cheeks are richer than lips or eyes) and weighting them accordingly.

Strength: typically the most accurate of the three on clean, well-lit face video.

What does ".rlap" mean?Each model name ends with .rlap. That is the training dataset tag — these weights were trained on the RLAP dataset, a large public rPPG dataset of face videos recorded alongside real contact-sensor pulse readings. So all three models learned from real ground-truth data. More on datasets in §14.

What each model returns

Running one model
mdl.process_faces_tensor(faces.copy(), fps=effective_fps)

bvp_i, _ = mdl.bvp()               # the pulse waveform (a long list of numbers)
hr_i     = mdl.hr(return_hrv=True) # { "hr": 72.4, "SQI": 0.81, "hrv": {...} }
  • BVP — the extracted pulse waveform.
  • HR — heart rate in beats per minute.
  • SQI — Signal Quality Index: how trustworthy this model thinks its own reading is.
  • HRV — heart-rate variability statistics (SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50, LF/HF, breathing rate).

Why three models instead of one? (classic interview question)

🎯 Better accuracy

Different architectures make different mistakes. Averaging them cancels out individual errors — this is the standard "ensemble" idea in machine learning.

🛡️ Fault tolerance

Each model runs inside its own try/except. If one crashes, returns None, or produces NaN, it is skipped with a warning and the others continue.

📊 Quality awareness

Each reports its own SQI, so we know which answer to trust more rather than blindly averaging.

🔍 Explainability

Every model's HR and SQI is returned in the diagnostics, so a developer can see exactly what each one said and which was chosen as best.

Total failure caseIf all three models fail, the API raises HTTP 500 with "All ensemble models failed" — it never invents a number.

9 · Step 5 — Fusing the three signals

Now we have up to three pulse waveforms. We combine them into one — but not by a plain average. A model that reports poor quality should count less.

SNR-weighted fusion (lab_router.py)
total_snr   = sum(snr_list)                    # sum of all SQI values
weights_arr = [s / total_snr for s in snr_list]  # each model's share

min_len    = min(len(b) for b in bvp_list)     # trim all to the same length
bvp_signal = np.zeros(min_len)
for w, bvp_i in zip(weights_arr, bvp_list):
    bvp_signal += w * bvp_i[:min_len]          # weighted sum

A worked example

ModelSQI (quality)WeightInfluence on final wave
FacePhys0.800.80 / 1.60 = 0.5050%
PhysNet0.500.50 / 1.60 = 0.3131%
EfficientPhys0.300.30 / 1.60 = 0.1919%

The clearest signal contributes most. A safety floor of max(sqi, 0.01) stops any model getting a zero or negative weight, and if the total quality is essentially zero the code falls back to a plain median.

Why trim to the shortest length?Different architectures can output slightly different-length waveforms (some drop a few frames at the edges). To add them together they must line up, so all are cut to the shortest one — losing at most a few frames out of hundreds.

10 · Step 6 — Heart rate and HRV

Heart rate comes from the best model, not the blend

For the waveform we blend all three. But for the heart-rate number we take the reading from the single highest-SQI model.

Picking the best model
best_idx   = int(np.argmax(snr_list))       # index of highest SQI
best_model = list(models.values())[best_idx]
hr_result  = best_model.hr(return_hrv=True)

hr_bpm   = float(hr_result.get("hr", 0.0))
hrv_data = hr_result.get("hrv", {})
sqi      = float(np.mean(snr_list))          # overall confidence = mean SQI

Why? Heart rate is a single precise number derived from peak timing. Averaging three heart rates — say 68, 72 and a noisy 95 — would drag the answer toward the bad one. Taking the cleanest model's reading is more reliable. (The waveform is different: blending waves genuinely reduces noise.)

What is HRV, and why do we return it?

HRV = Heart Rate Variability — the tiny differences in time between one beat and the next. A healthy heart is not perfectly regular; some variation is a sign of good autonomic health.

MetricWhat it means (simple)
SDNNOverall spread of beat-to-beat times. General variability.
RMSSDShort-term variation between neighbouring beats. Linked to the "rest and digest" nervous system.
pNN50Percentage of beat gaps differing by more than 50 milliseconds.
LF/HFBalance of low- vs high-frequency rhythms — often read as a stress indicator.
Breathing rateBreaths per minute, estimated from slow waves in the pulse signal.

The quality gate

Reject bad scans honestly
if sqi < 0.1:
    raise HTTPException(
        status_code=422,
        detail=f"Signal quality too low (SQI={sqi:.3f}). "
               "Try better lighting and hold still."
    )

If the average quality is below 0.1 the request is rejected with advice. The system never returns a guessed number from an unusable scan.

11 · Step 7 — The ResNet blood-pressure model ⭐

We now have a clean pulse waveform. Turning that into blood pressure is the job of a 1D-ResNet — a deep neural network pretrained on MIMIC-III hospital data. File: ml/src/lab_bp_model.py, weights: 1d_resnet_bp.pth (≈23 MB).

How can a pulse wave reveal blood pressure?

The shape of each pulse carries the answer. When arteries are stiff and pressure is high, the wave rises sharply and the reflected "bounce" (the dicrotic notch) sits differently than in a relaxed, low-pressure artery. Humans cannot reliably read those subtleties — a neural network trained on hundreds of thousands of examples can.

Stage 1 — Clean the signal

Detrend + adaptive bandpass
sig = detrend(bvp_signal)                       # remove slow drift
sig = _adaptive_bandpass(sig, fs, detected_hr)  # keep only the pulse band

# then scale to 0..1
sig = (sig - sig.min()) / (sig.max() - sig.min())

The "adaptive" bandpass — a smart detail

A bandpass filter keeps frequencies inside a range and removes everything outside. A normal system uses a fixed range (0.7–3 Hz). This one is smarter: because we already know the heart rate from step 6, it narrows the filter tightly around it.

Adaptive filter window
if detected_hr and 40 < detected_hr < 200:
    hr_hz = detected_hr / 60.0          # e.g. 72 bpm → 1.2 Hz
    low   = max(0.5, hr_hz - 0.5)       # → 0.7 Hz
    high  = min(fs/2 - 0.1, hr_hz + 0.5)# → 1.7 Hz
else:
    low, high = 0.7, 3.0                # fallback: the generic band

b, a = butter(4, [low/nyq, high/nyq], btype="band")
return filtfilt(b, a, sig)

Instead of keeping everything from 0.7–3 Hz, it keeps only 0.7–1.7 Hz — throwing away far more noise while keeping all of the real pulse. filtfilt runs the filter forwards and backwards so the wave is not shifted in time (important, since timing is the signal).

Stage 2 — Sliding windows

The network expects exactly 437 samples at a time. So a window of 437 samples slides along the signal, moving 80 samples each step.

Window loop
_WINDOW_LEN = 437
_STEP_SIZE  = 80

for start in range(0, len(sig) - 437 + 1, 80):
    window = sig[start : start + 437]
    q      = _window_quality(window, fs)          # 0..1 score for THIS window
    tensor = torch.tensor(window).unsqueeze(0).unsqueeze(0)  # → (1,1,437)

    with torch.no_grad():
        pred = model(tensor)                      # → [SBP, DBP]

    sbp_preds.append(float(pred[0, 0]))
    dbp_preds.append(float(pred[0, 1]))
    qualities.append(q)

Because the step (80) is much smaller than the window (437), the windows overlap heavily — each part of the signal is examined about 5 times from different alignments. That produces dozens of independent BP estimates instead of one.

Stage 3 — Quality-weighted averaging of windows

Each window gets its own quality score, computed with a Welch power spectrum: what fraction of that window's energy lies in the pulse band (0.7–3 Hz)? Mostly pulse = high score; mostly noise = low score.

Weighted combine, with a median fallback
q_sum = q_arr.sum()
if q_sum > 1e-8:
    weights  = q_arr / q_sum
    sbp_raw  = float(np.dot(weights, sbp_arr))   # clean windows count more
    dbp_raw  = float(np.dot(weights, dbp_arr))
else:
    sbp_raw  = float(np.median(sbp_arr))         # everything was noisy
    dbp_raw  = float(np.median(dbp_arr))

# Confidence range straight from the spread of window predictions
sbp_q05 = np.percentile(sbp_preds, 5)
sbp_q95 = np.percentile(sbp_preds, 95)
Where the 90% range comes fromNotice the interval is not guessed. If all windows agree closely, the 5th–95th percentile range is narrow → confident. If they disagree, the range is wide → the doctor immediately sees the reading is uncertain. This is honest uncertainty reporting.

12 · Step 8 — Blending with population averages (NHANES)

The final safety net. If the camera signal was weak, trusting the model 100% would be reckless. So the prediction is mixed with what is statistically normal for someone of that age and sex.

What is NHANES?

NHANES = the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, run by the CDC. It measures the real blood pressure of thousands of Americans. From it we get: "the average 45-year-old man has about 124.7 / 77.2 mmHg."

The built-in NHANES table (CDC 2017–2020 means)
MALE:                        FEMALE:
  age 18 → 117.5 / 68.8        age 18 → 110.8 / 66.2
  age 25 → 119.4 / 70.1        age 25 → 112.3 / 67.8
  age 35 → 121.8 / 74.5        age 35 → 114.9 / 71.2
  age 45 → 124.7 / 77.2        age 45 → 120.1 / 74.1
  age 55 → 129.3 / 78.1        age 55 → 127.8 / 75.3
  age 65 → 133.9 / 74.8        age 65 → 134.2 / 72.1
  age 75 → 137.2 / 70.4        age 75 → 139.1 / 68.3
  age 85 → 139.5 / 67.8        age 85 → 142.8 / 65.7

Ages in between are LINEARLY INTERPOLATED.
Example, age 40 (male): halfway between 121.8 and 124.7 → 123.25

BMI adjustment

Body weight shifts the baseline
bmi = weight / (height/100)**2

if bmi > 25:            # overweight → higher expected BP
    sbp += (bmi - 25) * 0.7
    dbp += (bmi - 25) * 0.35
elif bmi < 18.5:        # underweight → lower expected BP
    sbp -= (18.5 - bmi) * 0.5
    dbp -= (18.5 - bmi) * 0.3

The blend formula

Quality decides how much to trust the camera
blend = min(0.85, max(0.0, signal_quality))   # capped at 0.85

sbp_final = blend * sbp_from_resnet + (1 - blend) * sbp_from_nhanes
dbp_final = blend * dbp_from_resnet + (1 - blend) * dbp_from_nhanes

used_fallback = blend < 0.5      # reported as signal_source: "fallback"
Signal qualityBlendWhat the answer is really based on
0.85 or higher0.8585% camera, 15% population average — a clean scan.
0.600.6060% camera, 40% population average.
0.300.3030% camera, 70% population — flagged as fallback.
Why cap the blend at 0.85?Even a perfect-looking camera signal never gets 100% trust. Camera-based BP is an estimate, not a cuff measurement, so the population prior always keeps at least 15% influence. This deliberately pulls wild predictions back toward reality — a safety design choice, and a very good thing to say in an interview.

Everything is transparent in the response: model_info returns the raw ResNet numbers, the NHANES numbers, and the exact blend used — so nothing is hidden.

Every ensemble model name ends in .rlap, which identifies the training set. RLAP recordings pair face video with a real contact pulse sensor running at the same moment. That gives the network the perfect teaching signal: "here is the video, and here is the true pulse wave you should output."

2 · MIMIC-III — for the blood-pressure ResNet

MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care) is one of the most-used public medical datasets in the world. It contains de-identified records from tens of thousands of ICU stays, including continuous waveform recordings.

Why it is ideal for BP trainingICU patients have an arterial line — a catheter measuring blood pressure continuously and precisely, beat by beat — while a PPG sensor records their pulse wave. That is exactly what a BP model needs, and no camera dataset can offer it at that scale or precision.

The ResNet was pretrained on those PPG-waveform → (SBP, DBP) pairs. In MediIQ we then feed it a camera-derived pulse wave that has been filtered and normalised into the same shape the model expects.

15 · The ResNet — architecture and training

What is a ResNet, in simple words?

ResNet = "Residual Network". Deep networks (many layers) learn better but historically got worse past a certain depth, because the learning signal faded as it travelled back through the layers.

ResNet fixed this with a shortcut (skip) connection: the input of a block is added directly to its output. So each block only needs to learn the change ("residual") rather than rebuild everything, and the learning signal has a clear express lane back through the network.

The residual block (lab_bp_model.py)
def forward(self, x):
    identity = x                                  # remember the input
    out = self.relu(self.bn1(self.conv1(x)))      # conv → norm → activate
    out = self.bn2(self.conv2(out))               # conv → norm
    if self.downsample:
        identity = self.downsample(x)             # match shapes if needed
    out += identity                               # ★ THE SKIP CONNECTION
    return self.relu(out)

Why "1D"?

Image ResNets are 2D (height × width). Our input is a signal — a single line of numbers over time. So every convolution is Conv1d: filters slide along time only, learning shapes like "sharp rise", "wide plateau", "second bump".

The exact architecture

_ResNet1D — layer by layer
INPUT: (1, 1, 437)          ← 1 sample, 1 channel, 437 time steps

layer0:  Conv1d(1 → 64, kernel=7, stride=2, padding=3)
         BatchNorm1d(64) → ReLU
         MaxPool1d(3, stride=2, padding=1)         # shrink, keep strongest

layer1:  3 × ResidualBlock(64  → 64)
layer2:  3 × ResidualBlock(64  → 128, stride=2)    # halve length, double depth
layer3:  3 × ResidualBlock(128 → 256, stride=2)
layer4:  3 × ResidualBlock(256 → 512, stride=2)

global_pool: AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1)                  # 512 channels → 512 numbers
fc:          Linear(512 → 2)

OUTPUT: [SBP, DBP]          ← two numbers, in mmHg
PieceWhat it does (plain words)
Conv1dSlides a small pattern-detector along the signal looking for shapes.
BatchNorm1dKeeps numbers in a sensible range between layers so training stays stable.
ReLUTurns negatives into zero — lets the network model non-straight-line relationships.
MaxPool1dKeeps only the strongest value in each small region — shrinks the data, keeps the important bits.
stride=2Skips every other step, halving the length — the network sees wider patterns as it goes deeper.
AdaptiveAvgPool1d(1)Collapses each of the 512 channels to a single average, giving one fixed-size summary of the whole window.
Linear(512 → 2)The final layer that turns that summary into exactly two numbers: SBP and DBP.

Total depth: 12 residual blocks (3+3+3+3), each with 2 conv layers, plus the stem and the output layer — roughly a ResNet-26 style network, ~23 MB of weights.

How it was trained (on MIMIC-III)

1
Collect aligned pairs

From MIMIC-III: PPG waveform segments alongside the true arterial blood pressure at that moment.

2
Cut into fixed windows

Each PPG segment is sliced into 437-sample windows — which is why inference uses exactly 437 too.

3
Normalise each window

Detrended, bandpass-filtered and scaled to 0–1, so the network learns from shape, not from raw amplitude (which varies by sensor).

4
Train as a regression

Target = the true [SBP, DBP]. The network minimises the error between its 2 outputs and the truth, adjusting weights by backpropagation over many epochs.

5
Save the weights

Exported as 1d_resnet_bp.pth and loaded in MediIQ with weights_only=True (safer — it loads only numbers, never code).

How MediIQ loads itThe model is built once and cached in a module-level global, then set to .eval() mode (switches off training-only behaviour). All predictions run inside torch.no_grad() — no gradients are computed, which makes inference faster and lighter.

16 · The legacy XGBoost system (System A)

Still fully working and worth understanding — it shows a completely different ML approach to the same problem.

Its rPPG method: the POS algorithm

POS = Plane-Orthogonal-to-Skin (Wang et al., IEEE TBME 2017). It is pure mathematics — no learning, no training. It exploits a physical fact: blood-volume colour changes lie in a specific direction in RGB colour space, while lighting/motion noise lies in a different direction. Project along the right plane and the pulse survives while noise cancels.

POS in 5 steps (ml/src/pos_algorithm.py)
# Step 1 — remove slow lighting drift (per colour channel)
C_n = sig / sig.mean(axis=0) - 1.0

# Step 2 — project onto two skin-orthogonal planes
X = R_n - G_n
Y = 0.5*R_n + 0.5*G_n - B_n

# Step 3 — combine them, scaled so both contribute fairly
alpha = std(X) / std(Y)
raw   = X + alpha * Y

# Step 4 — bandpass 0.7–4 Hz  (= 42–240 bpm, the possible human range)
filtered = filtfilt(butter(4, [0.7/nyq, 4.0/nyq], 'band'), raw)

# Step 5 — normalise to zero mean, unit variance
return (filtered - mean) / std

Its face handling: 3 skin patches

Instead of the whole face, System A samples three specific regions using MediaPipe FaceMesh landmarks:

# Then a YCrCb skin-colour mask removes non-skin pixels

# ROI discarded if fewer than 50 valid skin pixels survive

Forehead and cheeks are chosen because they are flat, well-lit, and rich in blood vessels, with no hair or beard. The YCrCb skin mask is a colour-space trick: YCrCb separates brightness (Y) from colour (Cr, Cb), so skin can be detected reliably even when lighting changes. Any hair, glasses, or shadow pixels inside the region get filtered out.

Its full inference chain

The most important line thereStep 3, the resample from 30 Hz to 1000 Hz. The XGBoost models were trained on 1000 Hz finger recordings, and features like rise-time and pulse-width are measured in samples. Feeding a 30 Hz signal without resampling would make every timing feature ~33× wrong and the predictions meaningless.

Stage 2 — Train 6 models (train.py)

Why 6? Two targets (SBP, DBP) × three quantiles (5th, 50th, 95th percentile).

Model filePredicts
sbp_q05.pklLow end of the systolic range
sbp_q50.pklThe systolic answer (median)

dbp_q05 / q50 / q95.pklSame three for diastolic
What is quantile regression?A normal model predicts one number — the average. Quantile regression predicts a percentile. The q05 model learns "5% of real values fall below this", q95 learns "95% fall below this". Together they form a 90% prediction interval. Saying "≈124, likely 117–132" is far more honest than "124.0".

Exact hyper-parameters (train.py)
objective             = "reg:quantileerror"  # optimise a percentile
quantile_alpha        = 0.05 / 0.50 / 0.95   # which percentile
max_depth             = 5      # shallow trees → less over-fitting
n_estimators          = 400    # up to 400 trees
learning_rate         = 0.03   # small steps → careful learning
subsample             = 0.8    # each tree sees 80% of rows
colsample_bytree      = 0.8    # each tree sees 80% of features
reg_alpha             = 0.1    # L1 penalty (drops useless features)
reg_lambda            = 1.0    # L2 penalty (keeps weights small)
early_stopping_rounds = 30     # stop if 30 rounds bring no improvement
tree_method           = "hist" # fast histogram-based splitting
random_state          = 42     # reproducible results

Split: 80% train / 20% test  (train_test_split, seed 42)

Why each setting, in plain words

  • Shallow trees (depth 5) — with only 219 rows, deep trees would memorise individuals instead of learning patterns.
  • Slow learning (0.03) + many trees (400) — many small careful corrections beat a few big reckless ones.
  • Subsample + colsample (0.8) — showing each tree a random 80% of rows/columns forces variety, so trees don't all make the same mistake.
  • reg_alpha / reg_lambda — penalties that punish complexity, pushing the model to stay simple and generalise.
  • Early stopping (30) — watches the unseen test set and stops the moment it stops improving, preventing over-fitting.

18 · The 15 PPG features explained

These are the numbers System A measures from a pulse wave. (System B's ResNet skips this — it learns its own internal features from the raw wave.)

Heart-rate features (3)

FeatureHow it is computedWhy it matters
hr_fftRun an FFT (frequency analysis), find the strongest frequency between 0.7–4 Hz, × 60.Robust to individual weird beats — looks at the overall rhythm.
hr_peaksDetect peaks (min 400 ms apart, above the median), average the gaps, 60 ÷ gap.Direct beat counting — accurate on clean signals.
hr_finalAverage of the two above.Combines both methods for stability.

Heart-rate-variability features (3)

FeatureMeaning
rmssdRoot-mean-square of successive beat-gap differences — short-term variability.
sdnnStandard deviation of all beat gaps — overall variability.
pnn50% of consecutive beat gaps differing by more than 50 ms.

Shape (morphology) features (6) — the real BP signal

FeatureWhat it measuresLink to blood pressure
pulse_widthWidth of the pulse at 50% of its height.Narrower pulses often indicate stiffer arteries / higher BP.
rise_timeTime from the foot of the wave to its peak.How fast the heart ejects blood — related to contractility.
fall_timeTime from peak back down to the next foot.How quickly blood drains — reflects vascular resistance.
peak_amplitudeHeight of the pulse.Related to stroke volume (blood pushed per beat).
notch_ratioHeight of the dicrotic notch relative to the pulse.The notch is the small bump from the aortic valve closing and the reflected wave — strongly tied to arterial stiffness.
aug_indexAugmentation index: how much the reflected wave boosts the main wave.A classic clinical marker of arterial stiffness and hypertension.
How the dicrotic notch is foundThe code takes the second derivative of the falling part of each pulse (the "acceleration PPG"). The first point where it turns positive is the notch — a real trick used in clinical PPG analysis. Values are sanity-checked, and pulses with amplitude below 0.05 are skipped as too noisy.

Quality features (3)

FeatureMeaning
signal_qualityFraction of total power sitting in the pulse band (Welch PSD). Near 1 = clean.
peak_countHow many beats were detected — too few means unreliable.
signal_stdOverall variation — a flat line means no real signal.

Every feature is computed per pulse and then averaged, and any that cannot be computed becomes NaN — which XGBoost handles natively (it learns which branch to send missing values down).

22 · Limitations (state these honestly)

  • Not a medical device. These are estimates to support a doctor, never a replacement for a cuff.
  • Domain gap. The ResNet learned from contact ICU sensors but runs on camera signals — mitigated by NHANES blending, not eliminated.
  • Lighting sensitive. Dim or flickering light lowers SQI, pushing the answer toward the population average.
  • Motion sensitive. The patient must stay reasonably still for 45 seconds.
  • Skin tone & makeup can affect signal strength; heavy makeup or facial hair reduces usable skin area.
  • Blood pressure is genuinely hard. Heart rate from rPPG is well established; camera-based BP is still an active research area.
  • Small legacy training set (219 subjects) for System A.
  • Server required. The models run in a separate Python service, so the vitals feature needs that service reachable.

23 · Interview Q&A

Q Explain your vitals feature in 30 seconds. ›

A patient looks at their webcam for 45 seconds. In the browser, MediaPipe tracks 468 face landmarks and I crop 72×72 face thumbnails, which are gzipped and sent to a Python service — the video itself never leaves the device. There, an ensemble of three rPPG neural networks (FacePhys, PhysNet, EfficientPhys, all trained on RLAP) each extract a pulse waveform; I fuse them weighted by each model's signal-quality score. Heart rate and HRV come from the best model, and the fused waveform goes into a 1D-ResNet pretrained on MIMIC-III that predicts systolic and diastolic BP over overlapping windows. Finally I blend with NHANES population averages based on signal quality, and classify using AHA 2017 rules.

Q Which datasets did you use? ›

Four. RLAP — face videos with ground-truth contact PPG — trained the three ensemble rPPG models. MIMIC-III — ICU PPG waveforms paired with continuous arterial blood pressure — pretrained the 1D-ResNet BP model. PPG-BP — 219 subjects, 657 fingertip recordings at 1000 Hz with cuff BP labels — trained my six XGBoost quantile models in the legacy pipeline. And NHANES (CDC) provides population BP averages by age and sex, used as a statistical prior rather than for training.

Q Why an ensemble of three models? ›

Three reasons. Accuracy — different architectures fail differently, so blending cancels individual errors. Robustness — each runs in its own try/except, so a crash or NaN from one doesn't kill the request. Quality-awareness — each returns its own SQI, so I weight the good signals higher instead of blindly averaging. I also take the heart rate from the single best model rather than the blend, because averaging a noisy HR would drag the number off.

Q How does a pulse waveform give blood pressure? ›

Through the shape of each pulse. Arterial stiffness and pressure change how sharply the wave rises, how wide it is, and where the dicrotic notch (the reflected-wave bump) sits. In the legacy system I measured that explicitly as features like rise_time, notch_ratio and augmentation index. In the current system the ResNet learns those patterns itself from the raw waveform, having been trained on MIMIC-III where each waveform had a true arterial BP label.

Q What is quantile regression and why use it? ›

Instead of predicting the mean, each model predicts a percentile. I train separate models for the 5th, 50th and 95th percentile of SBP and DBP — six in total. The 50th is the answer; the 5th and 95th form a 90% prediction interval. For a medical estimate that's much more honest than a single number, and I validate it with a coverage metric: the true value should fall inside the interval about 90% of the time.

Q Why blend with NHANES? Isn't that cheating? ›

It's Bayesian thinking: combine the evidence with a sensible prior, weighted by how strong the evidence is. If signal quality is 0.8 I use 80% model and 20% population average; if it's 0.3, the population average dominates and I flag signal_source: "fallback" so the doctor knows. I also cap camera trust at 0.85 so a wild prediction can never run away unchecked. It's fully transparent — the raw model output, the NHANES value and the blend ratio are all returned in the response.

Q Why average frames in pairs? ›

Sensor noise is random, so averaging two neighbouring frames partly cancels it, while the pulse signal — nearly identical across 33 ms — survives. It halves the effective frame rate to 15 fps, but by Nyquist that still supports up to 450 bpm, far beyond any human heart rate. So it's free noise reduction. Importantly, the halved effective_fps is then used for every downstream timing calculation.

Q What's a ResNet and why 1D? ›

A ResNet adds skip connections that pass a block's input straight to its output, so each block only learns the residual change and gradients flow cleanly through deep networks. Mine has 12 residual blocks, widening 64→128→256→512 channels, then global average pooling and a linear layer producing two numbers. It's 1D because the input is a time signal, not an image — convolutions slide along time only, learning shapes like sharp rises and secondary bumps.

Q Biggest technical challenge here? ›

Making a ~1% signal survive real-world conditions. My answers were layered: capture longer (45s) and bigger crops (72×72) for more information; average frame pairs to cut sensor noise; use three models instead of one; weight everything by measured quality rather than trusting it equally; narrow the bandpass adaptively around the already-detected heart rate; and average across dozens of overlapping windows. Then, rather than pretending the result is perfect, I quantify uncertainty with a percentile interval and blend toward a population prior when quality is low.

Q How do you handle privacy? ›

Face detection and cropping run entirely in the browser using MediaPipe. Only 72×72 face thumbnails — gzipped — are transmitted; the video, audio and background never leave the device. On the server the frames stay in memory for a few seconds and are never written to disk, and the database stores only the resulting numbers, never image data.

Q How accurate is it, honestly? ›

Heart rate lands within roughly ±5–8 bpm in decent lighting, which is consistent with published rPPG results. Blood pressure is harder and I present it as an estimate with a 90% interval, not a precise reading — a wide interval is itself the signal that the scan was uncertain. It's designed to support a doctor's judgement, not to replace a cuff, and it is not a certified medical device.
