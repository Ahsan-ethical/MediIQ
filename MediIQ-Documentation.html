MediIQ — Healthcare that reasons with you

MediIQ is an online doctor-consultation platform. A patient does a live video call with a real doctor, and behind the scenes a team of 9 AI agents reads the patient's history, listens to the call, finds similar past cases, checks medical knowledge, and writes a safety-checked prescription draft for the doctor to review. It can even measure blood pressure and heart rate from a face video — no smartwatch needed.

🤖 9-agent AI pipeline 📹 Live video calls ❤️ Camera-based vitals 🔍 RAG + vector search 💊 Drug & allergy safety checks 🌗 3 roles · full dashboards

This handbook explains the entire project from start to finish in very simple words. Every hard word is explained the moment it appears. Read it top-to-bottom, or jump to any section from the sidebar. It is written so that someone who has never seen this project can understand exactly how everything works — and so you can confidently answer any interview question about it.

Start Here

1 · Project Overview

MediIQ is a telemedicine platform. Telemedicine simply means seeing a doctor over the internet instead of in person. A patient opens the website, picks a doctor who is online, and has a live video call. That part alone is normal. What makes MediIQ special is the AI layer that helps the doctor at every step.

In one paragraph

A patient books a visit and fills a short form about their symptoms. The moment they lock that form, background AI agents start preparing: they read the patient's medical history, search a library of past cases that look similar, look up the illness in trusted medical databases, and study the patient's allergies. Then the video call happens. After the call ends, more AI agents take over: they turn the conversation into clean text, write a first-draft prescription, and run two automatic safety checks — one for dangerous drug combinations and one for allergy conflicts. If anything is unsafe, the AI rewrites the prescription. Finally, the human doctor reviews everything, edits if needed, and sends the final prescription as a clean PDF. Nothing reaches the patient without a human doctor approving it.

Who uses it?

🧑‍🦱 Patient

Books consultations, fills symptom forms, scans vitals with their camera, joins the call, and receives a prescription.

🩺 Doctor

Goes "Live", accepts patients, reads an AI briefing during the call, then reviews and signs the AI-drafted prescription.

🛡️ Admin

Approves new doctors, chooses which AI model the whole system uses, and watches health-trend analytics.

Key idea to rememberMediIQ is "AI-assisted", not "AI-replacing". The AI does the tiring background work (reading, searching, checking, drafting). The licensed human doctor always makes the final call. This is both safer and legally correct.

2 · Problem Statement & Goals

The problems it solves

  • Doctors have very little time per patient. They often can't read the full history or double-check every drug interaction. MediIQ does that reading and checking automatically.
  • Prescription mistakes are dangerous. A drug can clash with another drug, or trigger an allergy. MediIQ runs automatic safety checks on every draft.
  • Vitals usually need special devices. Blood pressure needs a cuff; heart rate needs a monitor. MediIQ estimates both from a 30-second face video using the phone/laptop camera.
  • Past knowledge is wasted. Every clinic sees the same illnesses again and again, but that experience is never reused. MediIQ stores anonymised past cases and retrieves similar ones to guide new decisions.

The goals

🎯 Save doctor time

Auto-summarise history, transcribe the call, and pre-draft the prescription.

🎯 Increase safety

Two independent AI safety agents catch drug and allergy problems before the doctor signs.

🎯 Make care accessible

Contactless vitals + video calls mean care from anywhere, no special equipment.

🎯 Learn from history

Reuse anonymised past cases with vector search so the system gets smarter over time.

5 · Architecture Overview

The system has four big parts that talk to each other. Think of it like a hospital: the reception (frontend), the staff office (backend), the records room (database), and the specialist team (AI).

1 · Frontend (what you see)

Next.js pages and React components for patients, doctors, and admins. Runs in the browser.

2 · Backend (the brain-stem)

Next.js API routes + a custom Node server running Socket.IO for real-time updates.

3 · Database (memory)

MongoDB Atlas stores users, appointments, cases, and AI results. Includes a vector index for search.

4 · AI & ML (the specialists)

The 9-agent pipeline (using Gemini/DeepSeek/Ollama) + a separate Python service for camera vitals.

One important design choiceInstead of the default Next.js server, MediIQ runs a custom server (server.ts) that hosts the website and a Socket.IO server together. This lets call state, doctor approvals, and notifications update instantly without the browser repeatedly asking "any news yet?".

6 · Roles & User Flows

Every account has one role: patient, doctor, or admin. The role decides which pages and API routes the user can reach. The role is stored inside the login token, so the server always knows who is asking.

P Patient flow ›
  1. Register & fill a health profile (age, blood group, known conditions, allergies, current medicines).
  2. Go to Find Doctors, see who is "Live", and book a consultation.
  3. Fill the pre-consultation form (symptoms, duration, severity) and optionally do a camera vitals scan.
  4. Press Lock details — this quietly starts AI agents 3, 4, 5 in the background.
  5. Join the video call once the doctor approves.
  6. Receive the prescription, and chat with the post-visit assistant about medicines.
D Doctor flow ›
  1. Register, submit credentials, and wait for admin approval.
  2. Toggle "Live" to start receiving consultation requests.
  3. Accept a patient; during the call, read the AI patient briefing (summary, likely conditions, suggested questions).
  4. End the call → land on the post-call review page.
  5. Watch the 9-agent pipeline run, then review the AI-drafted prescription and safety flags.
  6. Edit if needed, then sign & send the prescription to the patient.
A Admin flow ›
  1. Approve or reject new doctor sign-ups.
  2. Choose the global AI Engine mode (which model the whole system uses).
  3. Monitor the Outbreak Monitor — anonymised symptom & severity trends across all consultations.
  4. Use developer tools to inspect the agent pipeline and the vitals lab.

This is the single most important section to understand the whole app. It follows one consultation from booking to final prescription, step by step.

Patient picks a Live doctor. An Appointment record is created in the database.

2

3

4

5

Turns the raw conversation into clean, structured text. If there was no audio, it is skipped honestly (no fake data).

8

Agent 7 checks drug-drug interactions; Agent 8 checks allergy conflicts. If either flags a drug, the pipeline loops back to Agent 6 to pick a safe alternative (up to 2 times).

9

Detects any newly discovered allergy or long-term medicine to add to the patient's record.

10
Doctor reviews → signs → sends PDF

The doctor edits if needed and sends the final prescription. Flagged drugs are never included.

8 · Frontend

The frontend is what the user sees and clicks. It is built with Next.js App Router — pages live in folders under app/, and the folder name becomes the web address.

How pages are organised

  • app/(auth)/ — login & register pages.
  • app/patient/ — dashboard, find doctors, pre-consultation, consultation, AI consult, profile.
  • app/doctor/ — dashboard, appointments, consultation, post-call review, profile.
  • app/admin/ — overview, doctor approvals, AI engine, analytics, dev tools.

Design system & components

All buttons, cards, inputs, and badges come from a shared UI kit in components/ui. Colours are defined once as design tokens (like --brand, --surface) so light and dark mode just swap the token values. Animations use Framer Motion helpers kept in one file so motion feels consistent everywhere.

How the frontend talks to the backend

Pages call backend API routes using the browser's fetch() function. For example, the doctor's post-call page keeps calling an endpoint every few seconds to ask "what is the pipeline doing now?" and updates the progress bar with the answer. This repeated asking is called polling.

State managementMediIQ keeps things simple: it uses React's built-in state (useState, useEffect) instead of heavy external libraries. Live shared state (like call status) comes through Socket.IO events, not a global store.

9 · Backend & Real-Time Layer

API routes

Every backend action is an API route — a small function on the server that the frontend can call. They live under app/api/. Examples:

RoutePurpose
/api/auth/loginCheck email + password, hand back a login token.
/api/doctors/availableList all approved doctors for the "Find Doctors" page.
/api/appointmentsCreate a new booking.
/api/appointments/[id]/lock-preconsultationLock the form and start pre-warming agents 3/4/5.
/api/appointments/[id]/run-precallActually run agents 3/4/5 and wait for them (serverless-safe).
/api/appointments/[id]/end-callMark the call finished and save the transcript.
/api/appointments/[id]/run-pipelineRun the post-call agents (2 → 6 → 7 → 8 → 9).

The custom server & Socket.IO

A file called server.ts starts Next.js and a Socket.IO server at the same time. Socket.IO keeps an open "phone line" between browser and server. When a doctor accepts a patient, the server instantly pushes a message down that line, and the patient's screen updates without a refresh.

Serverless gotcha (great interview point)On Vercel (where the app is deployed), a server function is frozen the moment it sends its reply. So you cannot "reply now, keep the AI running in the background" — the background work gets killed. MediIQ fixes this by making the pipeline routes wait for the AI to finish before replying (with an extended time limit), and by using an atomic claim in the database so the pipeline runs exactly once even though the page keeps polling.

10 · Authentication & Security

Authentication means "proving who you are". MediIQ uses JWT (JSON Web Token) stored in a browser cookie.

Login flow, step by step

1
Register

The password is hashed with bcrypt — scrambled one-way so even the database never sees the real password.

2
Login

The server compares the typed password against the stored hash. If they match, it creates a JWT.

3
Token stored

The JWT (holding user id, role, name) is put in a cookie named mediiq_token, valid for 7 days.

4
Every request

The server reads the cookie, verifies the token's signature, and now knows exactly who is asking and what role they have.

🔒 What a JWT is

A signed ticket. "Signed" means the server can detect if anyone changed it. If tampered, it is rejected.

🔒 Role checks

Each protected route checks the role — e.g. only a patient can list doctors, only the matching doctor can send that appointment's prescription.

🔒 Passwords

Never stored in plain text. bcrypt hashing makes stolen data useless to attackers.

🔒 Secrets

API keys and the JWT secret live in environment variables, never in the code or the Git repo.

Relationships (how records connect)

Real bug this project hit (good story for interview)When one API only imported DoctorProfile but not User, the "populate" step crashed on fresh serverless instances ("Schema not registered for model User"), so the doctor list showed empty until you refreshed. The fix: make each model register the models it points to so populate always works. A textbook serverless + Mongoose lesson.

The special vector index

The Case collection has an extra field called embedding — a long list of numbers describing the meaning of the case's symptoms. MongoDB Atlas builds a vector index (case_vector_index) on it so the AI can find "cases that mean something similar" very fast. This is the heart of the RAG search (Section 15).

12 · AI Engine & Modes

An LLM (Large Language Model) is an AI trained on huge amounts of text that can read and write like a person. MediIQ can use three different LLMs, and the admin picks which one the whole system uses. This choice is saved in the database, so it applies everywhere instantly.

The three models

Gemini

Google's model (gemini-2.5-flash). Strong medical reasoning. Also makes the search embeddings.

DeepSeek

DeepSeek v4 Flash, used through the hosted OpenCode Zen API. No local setup needed.

Ollama

qwen3:8b running on a local machine — full privacy, no cloud.

The four modes

ModeHow it behaves
Gemini OnlyEvery agent uses Gemini.
DeepSeek OnlyEvery agent uses DeepSeek.
Gemini + OllamaHybrid — the prescriber (Agent 6) prefers Gemini; all other agents use Ollama.
DeepSeek + GeminiHybrid — the prescriber prefers Gemini; all other agents use DeepSeek.

Why the prescriber is special

Writing the actual prescription (Agent 6) is the most safety-critical step, so in hybrid modes MediIQ gives it the best available model (Gemini). It does this with a clever race: it asks both models at the same time and prefers Gemini if it answers within 60 seconds; otherwise it uses the other model's answer so the patient is never left waiting. Every other (less critical) agent just uses the secondary model to save cost.

Permanent exceptionThe embedding model is always Gemini (gemini-embedding-001), no matter the mode. This is because the vector search index was built with Gemini's 3072-number embeddings, and search only works if the query uses the same embedding model.

13 · The 9 AI Agents (in full detail)

An agent here is a small, focused AI program that does one job, takes clear inputs, and returns a clear output. Instead of one giant AI doing everything, MediIQ uses 9 specialists in a chain. This is easier to build, test, cache, and trust. Each agent below lists its purpose, input, output, how it works, and who it feeds.

1 Patient Summary Agent ›

Purpose: Give the doctor a quick summary of who this patient is.

Input: the patient's profile + their past consultation history.

Output: a profile summary (general background) and a running clinical summary (what has happened across past visits).

How it works: runs when the profile is saved. It feeds the doctor's in-call briefing and Agent 6.

2 Transcribe Agent ›

Purpose: Turn the messy call conversation into clean, structured medical text.

Input: the raw call transcript.

Output: structured transcript + extracted symptoms, medicines, and allergies, with drug names normalised (standardised) using RxNorm.

How it works: runs after the call. Honesty rule: if there was no audio/speech, it does not invent anything — it marks itself "no audio detected" and the pipeline continues using the pre-consultation form instead.

3 Similar Cases Agent › RAG

Purpose: Find past patients whose symptoms look similar, to guide the current decision.

Input: current symptoms, duration, severity, patient age & conditions.

Output: the top ~10 most relevant past cases + a plain-language "pattern summary" of what those cases usually turned out to be.

How it works (this is the RAG engine — see Section 15): clean the symptom text → turn it into an embedding → vector-search the Case library → boost cases with matching age/conditions → ask the LLM to re-rank the best 10 → summarise. Feeds Agent 6.

4 Medical Context Agent › RAG

Purpose: Gather trusted medical knowledge about the patient's conditions/symptoms.

Input: the patient's conditions and booking symptoms.

Output: official ICD-10 codes (standard disease codes), patient-friendly explanations, and an evidence summary (findings, possible diagnoses, differentials, red flags).

How it works: looks up each condition's ICD-10 code (local table first, then NIH's API), pulls patient-friendly info from MedlinePlus, retrieves supporting evidence, and summarises it. Feeds Agent 6.

5 Allergy Context Agent ›

Purpose: Understand the patient's allergies deeply — not just the one drug, but the whole drug family.

Input: the patient's known allergies.

Output: each allergy expanded into its drug class, cross-reactivity warnings (related drugs that could also cause a reaction), and safe alternatives.

How it works: uses RxClass (a drug-classification database) to expand, e.g., "Penicillin allergy" into the whole penicillin/beta-lactam family. Feeds Agents 6 and 8.

6 Reasoning / Prescriber Agent › most important

Purpose: The actual decision-maker. Write the draft prescription.

Input: everything — patient profile, structured transcript (Agent 2), similar cases (Agent 3), medical context (Agent 4), allergy context (Agent 5).

Output: a diagnosis, a list of medicines (with dose, timing, frequency, duration, purpose), 5 advice points, and a drug-safety note — all as clean JSON.

How it works: a carefully written prompt tells it to reason independently (similar cases are "weak references", never to be copied), to avoid risky drugs for at-risk patients (e.g. no NSAIDs for diabetics), and to check interactions with current medicines. In hybrid modes, it prefers Gemini for quality. Feeds Agents 7 and 8.

7 Drug Interaction Check Agent › safety

Purpose: Catch dangerous drug-drug combinations and wrong doses.

Input: the draft prescription + patient's current medicines/conditions.

Output: a PASS/FLAG verdict for each medicine, with reasons.

How it works: checks interactions using the DDInter interaction database and verifies indications/doses via openFDA drug labels. Flags feed back to Agent 6.

8 Allergy Safety Check Agent › safety

Purpose: Make sure no prescribed medicine could trigger the patient's allergies.

Input: the draft prescription + the expanded allergy classes from Agent 5.

Output: PASS or a violation flag per drug (e.g. "Cephalexin cross-reacts with a Penicillin allergy").

How it works: cross-checks each drug's class (via RxClass) against the patient's allergy classes. Flags feed back to Agent 6.

9 Finding Detector Agent ›

Purpose: Keep the patient's record up to date automatically.

Input: the final pipeline output.

Output: new "findings" — e.g. an allergy or a long-term medicine mentioned during the visit — saved as pending for the patient to confirm.

How it works: writes findings to the SystemDetected collection so they can be added to the profile after approval.

14 · The Agent Pipeline (orchestration)

The pipeline is the manager that runs the agents in the right order and saves their results. It runs in two phases.

Phase 1 — Pre-warm (when the patient locks the form)

Agents 3, 4, 5 run in the background, in parallel (all at once, because they don't depend on each other). Their results are saved so they are ready before the call even ends. This makes the final prescription faster.

Phase 2 — Post-call (when the call ends)

A
Agent 2 transcribes

(or skips if no audio).

B
Make sure 3/4/5 exist

If the pre-warm didn't finish (very short call), the pipeline runs the missing ones now.

C
Agent 6 prescribes

Using all gathered data.

D
Agents 7 & 8 verify

If clean → done. If flagged → loop back to Agent 6 (max 2 loops) to replace unsafe drugs.

E
Agent 9 records findings, save final prescription

The doctor's page shows every result live.

The safety loop (great to explain in interviews)

This is the clever part. Agent 6 writes a prescription → Agents 7 & 8 judge it → if a drug is unsafe, the flags are sent back into Agent 6, which rewrites the prescription avoiding those drugs → checked again. This repeats up to 2 times, then anything still unresolved is escalated to the human doctor. Flagged drugs are never sent to the patient.

Reliability detailsEach agent's result is saved to the database the instant it finishes, so if the doctor refreshes the page, completed agents stay done and only unfinished ones restart. An atomic claim (a one-shot database flag) makes sure the pipeline runs exactly once even though the page polls every 2.5 seconds. Writes use "dot-notation" updates so one agent's save never erases another's.

15 · RAG Pipeline (similar-case retrieval)

RAG means Retrieval-Augmented Generation. In simple words: before the AI answers, first go fetch relevant real information, then let the AI use it. This makes answers grounded in real data instead of guesses. MediIQ's Agent 3 is a full RAG system over the clinic's Case library.

Step 0 — The data (the "knowledge")

The Case collection holds many anonymised past cases: symptoms, diagnosis, prescription, and patient demographics. Each case was turned into an embedding when it was stored.

What is an embedding?An embedding is a list of numbers (here, 3072 numbers) that represents the meaning of text. Two texts with similar meaning get similar number-lists, even if they use different words. "high temperature" and "fever" land close together. This lets the computer measure "meaning-closeness" mathematically.

Step 1 — Clean the symptom text

The patient's typed symptoms may have typos or slang. If the text looks messy, a quick LLM call cleans it into proper clinical words (e.g. "cant stop coughin" → "persistent cough"). If it already looks clean, this step is skipped to save time and money.

Step 2 — Turn the query into an embedding

The cleaned symptoms are sent to Gemini's embedding model, which returns the 3072-number vector. Importantly, only symptoms are embedded (not age/history), so matching is based on the illness itself.

Step 3 — Vector search in MongoDB Atlas

MongoDB compares the query vector against every case's stored vector using the case_vector_index, and returns the closest matches. It looks at 150 candidates and keeps the top 50.

Concept: the vector search (simplified)
$vectorSearch:
  index: "case_vector_index"
  path: "embedding"          // the 3072-number field on each Case
  queryVector: [ ...3072 numbers... ]  // the patient's symptoms
  numCandidates: 150         // scan 150 nearby cases
  limit: 50                  // keep the best 50

Step 4 — Demographic boost (re-ranking part 1)

Pure meaning-match isn't enough. A case from a patient with the same conditions or a close age is more useful, so MediIQ adds a small score boost to those and re-sorts. This is a simple, fast, rule-based re-rank.

Step 5 — LLM re-rank (re-ranking part 2)

The top 15 cases are shown to the LLM, which picks the 10 most clinically relevant and writes a one-line reason for each. This is smarter than pure math because it understands clinical nuance (e.g. differential diagnoses).

Step 6 — Build the summary (the "generation")

The LLM reads the final 10 cases and writes a short pattern summary: the most common diagnoses, the usual treatments, and any safety patterns. This summary + the 10 cases are handed to Agent 6 as a weak reference — helpful context, never a copy-paste answer.

Step 7 — Cache the result

The whole result is saved with a 30-day sliding cache. If a very similar patient appears again, the answer is returned instantly without redoing the search.

Why it is designed this wayReal medical guidance must be grounded in real cases and must not be blindly copied. So RAG retrieves real precedent, re-ranking picks the best, and the prompt firmly tells Agent 6 to reason independently. Retrieval gives knowledge; the prompt guards safety.

What is "ICD-10"? (you will be asked)

ICD-10 is a worldwide list of standard codes for diseases (e.g. a specific code for "influenza"). Using codes instead of free text means the system can talk about diseases precisely and pull the exact matching information.

17 · Camera Vitals — rPPG + Blood-Pressure Model

This is the most impressive part to explain in an interview, so it is covered in the most detail. The goal: measure heart rate and blood pressure from a 30-second face video, with no cuff and no wearable.

The core idea — what is rPPG?

rPPG stands for remote photoplethysmography. Long word, simple idea:

  • Every time your heart beats, it pushes blood into your face.
  • That extra blood changes the colour of your skin by a tiny amount — far too small for the eye to see, but a camera can measure it.
  • By watching how the skin colour rises and falls over time, we can recover your pulse waveform — the same wavy signal a fingertip pulse-oximeter produces.
Plain-word definitionPPG signal = a wavy line showing your pulse over time. rPPG = getting that same wavy line from a camera instead of a clip on your finger.

Part A — How the machine-learning model was MADE and TRAINED

Blood pressure is predicted by a trained ML model. Here is exactly how that model was built, in order.

1. The training data (what medical data was added)

The model was trained on a public medical dataset called the PPG-BP dataset. It contains real finger PPG recordings from many subjects, each labelled with that person's true systolic and diastolic blood pressure (measured with a real cuff). Each subject has multiple recordings. This gives pairs of "here is a pulse waveform" → "here is the real blood pressure", which is exactly what a model needs to learn from.

Two numbers in blood pressureSBP (systolic) = the higher number, pressure when the heart beats. DBP (diastolic) = the lower number, pressure when the heart rests. The model predicts both.

2. Turning waveforms into "features"

The model does not read the raw wavy line. Instead, each waveform is summarised into 15 meaningful numbers called features (a feature = one measurable property of the signal). These describe the shape and rhythm of the pulse:

Feature groupFeaturesMeaning (simple)
Heart ratehr_fft, hr_peaks, hr_finalHow fast the heart beats, measured two ways then averaged.
Heart-rate variabilityrmssd, sdnn, pnn50How much the time between beats changes — a health signal.
Pulse shape (morphology)pulse_width, rise_time, fall_time, peak_amplitude, notch_ratio, aug_indexThe exact shape of each pulse wave — strongly linked to blood pressure.
Qualitysignal_quality, peak_count, signal_stdHow clean/trustworthy the signal is.

Why features instead of the raw signal? Because these shape numbers (like how sharp the pulse rises, or the "dicrotic notch" caused by the artery's elasticity) carry the real information about blood pressure, and they let a simpler, faster, more reliable model do the job.

3. The model type — XGBoost quantile regression

XGBoost is a popular ML method that combines many small decision trees to predict a number. MediIQ actually trains 6 models:

  • 3 for SBP and 3 for DBP.
  • For each, one predicts the low estimate (5th percentile), one the middle (50th, the actual answer), and one the high estimate (95th percentile).
  • The low + high give a 90% confidence range — e.g. "systolic ≈ 122, likely between 114 and 130". Showing a range is more honest than a single fake-precise number.
Training config (from train.py) — explained below
objective     = "reg:quantileerror"   # predict a percentile, not just the mean
max_depth     = 5        # each tree is shallow (avoids over-fitting)
n_estimators  = 400      # up to 400 trees
learning_rate = 0.03     # learn slowly and carefully
early_stopping_rounds = 30   # stop if it stops improving
reg_alpha=0.1, reg_lambda=1.0   # penalties that keep the model simple
train/test split = 80% / 20%    # learn on 80%, grade on the unseen 20%

In simple words: it builds up to 400 tiny decision trees, learning slowly so it generalises well, checks itself on data it never saw, and stops early if it is no longer improving. The trained models are saved as .pkl files and loaded by the vitals service.

4. How the model is graded

It is scored with MAE (Mean Absolute Error = average mistake in mmHg) and coverage (how often the true value really falls inside the predicted 90% range). Heart rate lands within roughly ±5–8 beats per minute in good lighting.

Research/lab modelAlongside XGBoost, the project also experiments with a deep-learning model — a 1D-ResNet neural network (saved as a .pth file) — kept in a separate "lab" area for research. XGBoost is the production choice because it is fast, light, and reliable on this feature-based data.

Part B — How a live camera scan works (inference)

The training used clean finger data at 1000 samples/second. A webcam is much slower (~30 frames/second) and noisier, so the live pipeline has to carefully convert the camera signal to match what the model expects.

1
Capture face video (~30 fps)

The patient looks at the camera for ~30 seconds.

2
Find skin regions with MediaPipe

MediaPipe locates the face; the app samples 3 skin patches: forehead, left cheek, right cheek. It averages the colour in each patch per frame.

3
POS algorithm → pulse waveform

The POS (Plane-Orthogonal-to-Skin) algorithm combines the red/green/blue colour changes in a way that cancels out lighting noise and keeps the real pulse. Done for each of the 3 patches.

4
Combine the 3 patches by quality

Each patch gets a signal-quality score (SNR). Cleaner patches count more in the final combined waveform.

5
Resample 30 Hz → 1000 Hz

The camera waveform is stretched to 1000 samples/second so it matches the training data exactly. This step is critical — without it the features would be wrong.

6
Extract the 15 features

The same feature code used in training runs on the live waveform.

7
XGBoost predicts BP + HR

The 6 models output systolic & diastolic with a 90% range, plus heart rate and an AHA blood-pressure category (Normal / Elevated / High).

Part C — Privacy (a strong talking point)

No video ever leaves the deviceThe browser does the face-detection and colour-averaging locally, then sends only the tiny per-frame colour numbers (about 30 KB) to the Python vitals API. The actual video of the patient's face is never uploaded or stored. The server just runs POS → resample → features → prediction and returns the numbers.

Where the vitals service lives

The vitals system is a separate Python FastAPI service (in the ml/ folder). The main endpoint is /api/v1/vitals/predict-rgb. It validates the input (must be 3 equal-length patches, values 0–255, at least 60 frames), rejects low-quality scans with a helpful message ("please rescan in better lighting"), and returns the result. The Next.js app calls this service and attaches the vitals to the pre-consultation.

20 · Caching & Performance

AI calls and external medical APIs are slow and sometimes cost money. MediIQ avoids repeating work with several layers of caching (saving a result to reuse later).

Agent result caches

Agents 3, 4, 5, 7, 8 each have their own cache collection. A repeat of a similar request returns the saved answer instantly.

Sliding TTL

Caches expire after a period (e.g. 30 days), but each use extends the life — popular answers stay hot.

Pre-warming

Agents 3/4/5 run during the call, so their results are ready by the time it ends.

Parallel execution

Independent agents run at the same time instead of one after another.

Skip cheap LLM calls

Clean symptom text skips the "clean-up" LLM call entirely.

External-API cache

MedlinePlus/ICD lookups are cached in memory/DB to avoid re-hitting free APIs.

21 · Error Handling & Reliability

  • Honest empty states: if an agent finds nothing (no similar cases, no audio), it says so instead of inventing data.
  • allSettled for parallel agents: if one of agents 3/4/5 fails, the other two are still saved — one failure never wastes the others' work.
  • Model fallback: if the preferred model times out, the backup model answers so the user is never stuck.
  • Atomic claim: guarantees the pipeline runs exactly once despite constant page polling.
  • Serverless-safe DB connection: reuses the database connection only if it is truly alive, retries on failure, and never caches a broken connection — this fixed an intermittent "no data until refresh" bug.
  • Graceful API failures: external medical APIs have timeouts; if one is down, the agent continues with what it has.

24 · Limitations (be honest in interviews)

  • Not a medical device. It assists a doctor; it is not certified for real clinical use on its own.
  • Vitals are estimates. Camera BP/HR depends on good lighting and a still face; it is not a replacement for a real cuff.
  • LLMs can make mistakes. That is exactly why two safety agents + a human doctor review every prescription.
  • Case library is small. RAG quality grows as more anonymised cases are added.
  • Serverless time limits. Very slow model runs can hit the platform's function time cap.

27 · Interview Q&A — Quick Reference

Short, ready-to-say answers to the most likely questions.

Q In one line, what is MediIQ? ›

An AI-assisted telemedicine platform where a real doctor runs a video consultation and a 9-agent AI pipeline prepares context, drafts a safety-checked prescription, and even reads vitals from a face video — but the doctor always approves the final prescription.

Q How does RAG work here? ›

Symptoms are turned into a 3072-number embedding with Gemini, MongoDB Atlas vector-searches the case library for the closest cases, they're re-ranked (first by matching age/conditions, then by the LLM), summarised, and handed to the prescriber as grounding context — never copied.

Q How was the BP model trained? ›

On the public PPG-BP dataset (finger PPG waveforms labelled with real cuff BP). Each waveform becomes 15 shape/rhythm features. Six XGBoost quantile models (SBP & DBP × low/median/high) are trained with an 80/20 split, shallow trees, slow learning rate, regularisation, and early stopping — giving a BP estimate plus a 90% confidence range.

Q How do you get BP from a camera? ›

MediaPipe finds 3 skin patches (forehead + cheeks), the POS algorithm turns their tiny colour changes into a pulse waveform, patches are combined by quality, the signal is resampled to 1000 Hz to match training, 15 features are extracted, and XGBoost predicts BP + HR. Only colour numbers (not video) leave the device.

Q How do you make it safe? ›

Two independent AI agents check every draft — one for drug-drug interactions (DDInter/openFDA), one for allergy conflicts (RxClass). If anything is flagged, the prescriber rewrites it (up to 2 loops). Flagged drugs are never sent, and a licensed doctor signs off.

Q Biggest technical challenge? ›

Running a long multi-step AI pipeline on serverless, where background tasks get killed. Solved with awaited routes + extended time limits + an atomic once-only claim + saving each agent's result immediately so refreshes never lose progress.
